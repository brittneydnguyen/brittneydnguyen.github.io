Bio Teaching Research Writing

Learning is like making a good soup.

One of my favorite Vietnamese dishes is bún bò huế, a spicy beef and pork soup. Its primary ingredients are onions, lemongrass, shrimp paste, fish sauce, and—of course—lots of beef and pork cuts, all simmered together in a big pot for quite some time.

When you eat bún bò huế, you aren't necessarily tasting each of the individual components. Sure, some bits are more prominents than others: you might accidentally bite into a chunk of lemongrass, and you will fiend at the sight of a tender oxtail in your bowl. But you couldn't tell me that each bowl contains a teaspoon of fish sauce and 0.2783 of an onion. All of the flavors are married together in this rich and savory broth, such that together, they are greater than the sum of their parts.

Every educator knows this struggle: students do not see the relevance of most coursework towards their future. And students have a good point—when will they ever have to recall the steps of the citric acid cycle in "real" life? Goodness, do I even know the steps of the citric acid cycle? (The answer is no, even though I was a teaching assistant for a cellular biology class two quarters ago.) Some of the solution to this struggle is to make the material more relatable to students, of course. Provide those big picture connections to tangible phenomena. But we also need to address the root cause of this issue.

As a society, we have lost our collective idea of the purpose of an education. I believe that an education is not only meant to prepare students for a specific career, but also to provide them with the soft resources to be an independent, functioning, and—most importantly—kind individual who does good for our world. Every day, in every class, students learn something that shapes their personhood. The thriving adult they grow into out of school cannot emerge without these tiny details that are often shrugged off as extraneous, meaningless minutia. It is up to us teachers to change students' philosophy on education, to convince them that one day, all of this will be worth it. All of this will matter.

If I had no shrimp paste left in the fridge, you might not see the shrimp paste missing in the bún bò huế. But you would certainly think the soup is incomplete.

Materials

This final exhibition & reflection activity is designed for students to identify how their experiences season their intellectual and emotional identities, while also showing off the skills they learned in class. Students also played a direct role in the creation of this project by answering a survey, which constituted the deidentified data for this activity!

In addition, I believe that humor plays a huge part in keeping students engaged, which is why I taught students joins in R using hypothetical hamsters.
