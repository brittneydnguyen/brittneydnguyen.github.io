Bio Teaching Research Writing

Learning is like making a delicious soup.

One of my favorite Vietnamese dishes is bún bò huế, a spicy beef and pork soup. Its primary ingredients are onions, lemongrass, shrimp paste, fish sauce, and—of course—lots of beef and pork cuts, all simmered together in a big pot for quite some time.

When you eat bún bò huế, you aren't necessarily tasting each of the individual components. Sure, some bits are more prominents than others: you might accidentally bite into a chunk of lemongrass, and you will fiend at the sight of a tender oxtail in your bowl. But you couldn't tell me that each bowl contains an eighth of a teaspoon of fish sauce and 0.2783 of an onion. All of the flavors are married together in this rich and savory broth, such that together, they are greater than the sum of their parts.

Every educator knows this struggle: students do not see the relevance of most coursework towards their future. And students have a good point—when will they ever have to recall the steps of the citric acid cycle in "real" life? Goodness, do I even know the steps of the citric acid cycle? (The answer is no, even though I was a teaching assistant for a cellular biology class two quarters ago.) Some of the solution to this struggle is to make the material more relatable to students, of course. Provide those big picture connections to tangible phenomena. But we also need to address the root cause of this issue.

We have lost our collective idea of the purpose of an education. I believe that an education is not only meant to prepare students for a specific career, but also to provide them with the soft resources to be an independent, functioning, and—most importantly—kind individual who does good for our world. Every day, in every class, students learn something that shapes their personhood. The thriving adult they grow into out of school cannot emerge without these tiny details that are often shrugged off as extraneous, meaningless minutia. It is up to us teachers to change students' philosophy on education, to convince them that the things they do matter. They might not ever have to recite the steps of the citric acid cycle, but the diligence they demonstrated by studying hard and committing those steps to memory will be used over and over again.

If I had no shrimp paste left in the fridge, you might not see the shrimp paste missing in the bún bò huế. But you would certainly think the soup was incomplete.

Materials

BIOL 005B

In such a large public university such as UCR, I felt extremely lucky to have had only 25 students during my first instructor of record experience in Summer 2026. I sprung at the opportunity to modify existing BIOL 005B curricula to meet the needs of a small classroom by designing Activity Days that enabled students to learn experientially. Such Activity Days followed the 5E Model of Instructional Design. In this activity and paired slides, students played a game to learn about the trade-offs of different modes of reproduction, and conducted a little bit of research I did during my undergraduate career to learn about the nuances of sex determination!

The fastest way for a person to lose all sense of critical thinking is to make them apathetic enough as to no longer try. Whether in a class of 400 or 25, students thrive when they feel like they matter. Thus, I try to provide students with agency in showcasing what they learned. For their BIOL 005B final, students were able to choose between taking a traditional final exam or submitting a creative final project for BIOL 005B that summarizes a major topic in the course. Students had free reign in deciding what art they would make (some wrote short stories, other filmed funny TikToks, and one baked cupcakes!), but all students had to write a metacognitive reflection connecting their piece to what they learned and their interests.

As I alluded to prior, making material relatable to students is critical for keeping students engaged. That's why I conducted an experiment on myself to show students the physiological consequences of changes in your gut microbiome.

BIOL 101

If we're not having fun while we're learning, what even is the point of it all? To make my BIOL 101 class laugh a bit, I taught students joins in R using hypothetical hamsters.

My philosophy of learning being akin to making soup is something I repeatedly tell my students. This BIOL 101 final exhibition & reflection activity is designed for students to identify how their experiences season their intellectual and emotional identities, while also showing off the skills they learned in class. Students also played a direct role in the creation of this project by answering a survey, which constituted the deidentified data for this activity!
